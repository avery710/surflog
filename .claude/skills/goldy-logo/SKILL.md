# Goldy Logo

Make logos that survive a hard professional review, and produce them as actual editable
vector files. Not descriptions of logos, not raster mockups.

The standard here is deliberately strict, and it is grounded in a library of 713 real,
anonymous logo critiques (`references/critiques.md`). Be blunt and concrete about what is
broken and why; never abusive, never personal about the designer.

## Workflow

1. **State the brief.** One line on what this has to do. Ask at most three questions if the brief is thin.
2. **Explore.** At least five genuinely distinct directions, not five variations of one. The first sketch is rarely the answer; always make the second and third.
3. **Build.** Set the wordmark from a real typeface converted to outlines, construct the mark on one consistent geometry, assemble the lockup. See `references/production.md` and `scripts/`.
4. **Audit.** Run the gate below against the *rendered picture*, not the source. Non-negotiable.
5. **Deliver.** One recommendation, at most one deliberate alternative. Never a contact sheet of twenty.

For critique work instead of design work, skip to [Critiquing an existing logo](#critiquing-an-existing-logo).

## 1. Start with the brief

Every project starts with one line stating what this has to do. Without it nobody,
including you, can tell good work from bad: you can't judge whether a job succeeded if
nobody agreed what the job was.

Establish before drawing anything:

- Exact spelling and capitalization of the name; script(s) needed (Latin, Cyrillic, both).
- What the organization actually is, and its **character**: not its industry, its temperament.
- Where the mark will live: app icon, shop sign, packaging, letterhead, vehicle, embroidery, engraving.
- The smallest real size it must work at.
- Whether one-colour/black-and-white reproduction is required (it usually is).
- Fixed constraints from the client: mandated colour, a required letter, an existing element to keep.

Client constraints are normal, not an insult. If the client asks for blue and a letter G,
make a good blue logo with a G, then make another variant and explain why it is better.
Real constraints (cheap plastic, a low-refresh screen, a minimum legal type size) are the
job, not an obstacle to it.

If the brief is thin, ask up to three questions: name spelling, the smallest place it
will be used, and whether one colour is required. Then proceed; don't interrogate.

## 2. A logo identifies. It does not explain.

It is enough for a logo to be an identifier. Nobody can tell from the Sony logo or the
Nike swoosh what those companies make. What a mark carries is not *what* the company does
but *how* it does it: its character. Sony reads as precise, Nike as active.

- **Do not depict the industry.** A locomotive for a railway, a tooth for a dentist, a house for a builder: these are the default failures, not solutions.
- **Not a riddle.** The logo must not be a puzzle to solve or need an accompanying legend. If it takes a diagram to explain, it has failed. Everyone forgets the rationale a week after the presentation; only the mark remains.
- A mark that needs a story to stand up does not stand up.

## 3. One idea, clearly visible

A good logo has one main idea, few ingredients, and you can see the idea clearly. The
failure mode is the salad: a heart, a tooth, a shield, a cardiogram and a crown, all
diced fine and tossed together.

- State the idea in one short sentence. If it takes two, cut one.
- **Never enumerate.** Place logos that pile up local landmarks are the classic disaster: a list of images, not a logo.
- **Never say the same thing twice.** A lake-town logo that shows the lake's outline, a wave, a local animal, the lake's name and a blue stripe says one thing five times. Once is enough.
- Sometimes doing less makes it better. Decoration piled on past the point the idea needed it is the most common way a sound idea gets ruined.

## 4. Form carries more than subject

Think not only about *what* to depict but *how*. In most cases the styling and the
plastic form carry far more information than the subject.

**Plastic form** is the formal logic that ties a mark's parts into one coherent system:
smooth transitions, a shared logic of shape, a felt sense of motion. It is how one part
flows into the next so the whole reads as one thing, not a pile of unrelated details
bolted together.

A metaphor assembled mechanically is not a solution. When a flame is simply stuck on top
of a head, the literary idea exists but the form doesn't; nothing in the structure of the
drawing earns the combination.

There are exactly two routes to a good mark:

1. **An unexpected subject move**: a genuinely new angle on the thing.
2. **A beautiful form**: plastic quality that works on its own terms.

One of the two must really be present. If neither is, you have a placeholder, and you
should start over rather than polish it.

## 5. Every part must be a relative of every other part

The mark should look like a relative of the type, and the type a relative of the mark.

- Mark and wordmark share construction logic: stroke weight, contrast, terminal treatment, geometry, corner radius.
- **One device per logo.** If the mark is decorative and geometric, the wordmark must be plain. Two devices in one logo is one too many.
- Everything must be connected by form and by placement. A pile of unrelated elements sitting in different planes is not a mark.
- Do not break the logo into differently coloured parts. Multi-coloured letters inside one word are almost always damage.

## 6. Typography, where amateurs are exposed

The largest single category of logo failure.

- **Use a real, well-made typeface.** Do not draw your own letters unless you can actually draw letters; if you can't, use a type designer's work. When in doubt, take a neutral, well-built grotesque.
- **The amateur tell:** letters built out of circles, squares and triangles, and fear of stroke contrast and real curves.
- **Mutilating one letter to "make it a logo" is a reflex to resist.** Replacing the O with a target, tilting one letter, putting a leaf on the i: these mark the work as amateur instantly.
- **Optical compensation:** a horizontal stroke reads thicker than a vertical of the same width. Verticals must be drawn slightly heavier, horizontals slightly lighter.
- **Even colour:** squint at the wordmark. No zone may read darker or lighter than the rest. Uneven density is the most common lettering defect.
- **Rhythm:** letterspacing is even *optically*, not metrically. The failure looks like `F INNR O S T`. Watch for the word compressing toward its end.
- **Counters** are part of the letter and carry legibility. Do not fuse letters until their internal whites collapse.
- **Internal ties are always tighter than external ones.** If the line spacing inside the name exceeds the gap between name and mark, the composition is broken. Same rule for a letter's inner space versus its side bearings.
- **No tangents, no kisses,** no accidental dark spots where two strokes nearly touch. Leave a real gap or make a real overlap. A visible vector self-intersection is a manufacturing defect, not an artistic device.
- Lowercase reads open and contemporary; all-caps reads institutional. Choose on purpose.
- Punctuation is fine. A confident comma or apostrophe can carry a logo. Don't shrink it in embarrassment.
- **No ™ or ® in the artwork.** Serious companies stopped putting them in logos long ago.
- Don't add small dots, ticks or flourishes out of fear of looking like you did too little. Viewers sense that motive immediately.

## 7. It must survive the real world

- **The smallest real size is the primary view.** Judge it there first, not at 2000px.
- If a detail dies on reduction, exaggerate it deliberately, like stage makeup: overdone up close, correct from the audience.
- **Watch display faces at small sizes.** Heavy weights with ball terminals close their apertures: a lowercase c can read as an e at 13px. Test the actual size, and switch weight or case if it fails.
- **Snap to the pixel grid at small sizes.** For an outlined SVG at nav or favicon size, choose the viewBox so cap height and baseline land on whole pixels; otherwise every horizontal edge straddles two rows and looks grainy.
- **One colour must work.** Fax, stamp, embroidery, engraving, single-colour print, a sign at night.
- **Blur test:** look at it the way someone does from a passing bus: lean back, defocus. What survives is the logo. Parts that disappear (a pale letter, a thin stroke) are broken.
- **No gradients, drop shadows, gloss, bevels or reflections** as the load-bearing idea. Anyone can type a word and put a shadow under it; that isn't design. If flattening to a single flat colour kills it, the effect was the logo, and effects date within a couple of years.

## 8. Read it like a stranger, not like its author

The most frequent catch, and the most embarrassing one.

- **Does the wordmark accidentally read as another word?** A place name reading as a different place, letters resolving into an obscenity, a monogram reading as a vulgar syllable. Check every language the audience uses, including reading the letters as shapes.
- **Are the letters unambiguous?** p vs r, M vs CM, I vs l vs 1. Check numbers too: stylized digits can turn "58" into "1581", or a name into a date.
- **Does the mark read as an existing sign?** Parking, metro, radiation, medical, a competitor's mark. Never build on someone else's established symbol; viewers will see their symbol, not yours.
- **Does a geometric mark placed beside the wordmark get read as a letter of it?** A ring with a vertical bar in front of a word reads as Ф. A triangle before "ALTO" becomes an A. This is easy to miss because you know what you meant.
- **Does the subject contradict its own logic?** Football is foot + ball, so why hands? A horseshoe hung for luck points up. A hand drawn palm-out reads as a specific hand. Clip-art parts at impossible angles (a sword held in a way no hand can hold it, a head turned the wrong way) get caught immediately.
- Say it out loud. Read it upside down. Read only the first letters.

## 9. Freshness is mostly a list of things not to do

Worn-out motifs:

> heart (especially I♥CITY derivatives) · leaf or sprout for anything "eco" · water drop ·
> upward growth arrow · swoosh/tick · shield · ribbon banner · crown · curlicues and
> flourishes · little house with a roof · cogwheel · globe · low-poly polygon mesh ·
> QR/pixel mosaic · gradient mesh · drop shadow · gloss and highlights · a traced
> photograph · anything assembled from clip-art

Also beware the **universally safe moves**: paint it all one bright colour, set it in
oversized type, make it all stripes, use multicoloured dots. They always look acceptable
and have exactly one flaw: no individuality. Any company on earth could have it.

A cliché can be used in only two ways: with real delicacy, or boldly and on purpose.
Neutral, default use always loses.

Copying the *construction principle* of a famous logo is the same error as copying the
logo. Imitators end up sharing one look while the original moves on.

## 10. Process

- Explore at least five genuinely distinct directions before committing, not five variations of one.
- **Show one variant**, the one that answers the brief. Not a contact sheet of twenty. If it's rejected, rethink and bring the next one. (Internally, generate many; externally, present one, with at most one deliberate alternative.)
- **No fake construction grids.** Golden-ratio circles drawn over a finished mark to justify it mislead your own team. If you show geometry, it must be the geometry you actually built with.
- Before/after: old on the left, new on the right. Always.

## Execution

Deliver **SVG**: vector, self-contained, no external font dependency, no embedded
rasters, no filters.

Full production detail (font discovery, the outlining script, kerning and optical
tracking, mark construction, lockup rules, file specs) is in
**`references/production.md`**. Read it before writing any SVG.

The three scripts:

| Script | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `scripts/find-fonts.mjs` | Cross-platform font discovery. Replaces `fc-list`; works on Windows/macOS/Linux. |
| `scripts/wordmark.py` | Sets text in a real font and emits outlined SVG paths. Needs `fontTools`. |
| `scripts/proof-sheet.mjs` | Renders an SVG to the mandatory proof sheet PNG via headless Chrome. Replaces `cairosvg`. |

## The gate: mandatory self-audit before delivery

Run `scripts/proof-sheet.mjs` and **actually look at the PNG**. Do not audit the code,
audit the picture. Generated geometry fails in ways the source never reveals: an
off-by-one that silently drops a letter, a rule that collides with its neighbours, a
shape that reads as something else entirely. Reading the render letter by letter against
the intended spelling is the single highest-yield check in this document.

Then answer each question **in writing**. Any "no" means fix it or start another variant.
Shipping a known failure with a rationalization is the worst outcome this process exists
to prevent.

1. Does the rendered image spell the name, every letter present, in order?
2. Can I state the idea in one sentence, without a legend?
3. Is it an identifier of character rather than an illustration of the industry?
4. Is there either a real subject move or a genuinely good form, not neither?
5. Is there exactly one idea, not a mixture?
6. Are mark and type visibly relatives: same construction logic, one device?
7. Does the type come from a well-made face, with no mutilated letter used as a gimmick?
8. Is the colour even when I squint? Is the letterspacing optically even?
9. Are internal gaps tighter than external ones?
10. Any tangents, near-touches, dark junctions, or self-intersections?
11. Does it hold at the smallest real size?
12. Does it work in one flat colour, and inverted on black?
13. Does anything disappear when blurred?
14. Does it read as an unintended word, obscenity, number, or existing sign, in any relevant language?
15. Does it violate the real-world logic of its own subject?
16. Is it free of the cliché list, and of the "safe but characterless" moves?
17. Would this be recognizable a year from now, or is it a dated effect?

## What to deliver

- `logo.svg` (primary lockup), `logo-stacked.svg`, `mark.svg`, `logo-mono.svg` (one colour).
- A rendered test sheet (`proof-sheet.png`, or an HTML page): full size; 24px and 16px; black on white; white on black; one-colour silhouette; blurred; on a business card and a sign.
- A short written statement: the brief in one line, the idea in one sentence, which of the two routes it took, the audit results including anything that failed and why it's acceptable, and what changes at small size.

Do not deliver a contact sheet of twenty options. One recommendation, at most one
alternative, and say which you'd sign off on.

## Critiquing an existing logo

Same standard in reverse.

- **Anchor every remark to a specific region** of the image, e.g. "the junction of the R and the circle, upper left". State what is broken, why, and the concrete fix.
- Rank by severity: **idea failures first, then construction, then finish.**
- Run the same 17 questions above against the logo as it is, and report each failure.
- Say plainly when something works, and explain why. A harsh review that never names real quality isn't credible.
- Be direct and specific. Never profane, never abusive, never about the person.

To ground a critique in precedent, find comparable cases in the critique library. See
**`references/critiques.md`** for what's in it and how to search it. Citing a real case
where the same failure was called out ("Case 0108: …") is far stronger than asserting
the rule yourself. The same library is useful during design work too: before committing
to a motif or device, search it to see whether that move has already been criticised.
